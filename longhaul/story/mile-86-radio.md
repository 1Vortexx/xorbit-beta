# Mile 86 Radio
Two hour-long free-roam broadcasts for Long Haul

Mile 86 is a listener-supported station above a tyre shop. Host Inez Flores records on a portable cassette machine with a sticky pause key. Conversations run dry and close in the mix. Field recordings widen gently into stereo. Keep voices above engines. Keep horns, impacts, emergency tones and directions to the player out of the broadcast. All music named below is a proposed original cue.

These are timed production treatments with scripted anchor scenes and directed interview and documentary sequences. Each clock totals 60:00 including music and field recordings. Record and edit the interviews to the listed slots. The spoken excerpts alone are shorter than an hour.

## Episode one — A Receipt for a Yellow Door

**00:00–03:00 · Cold open.** A pencil scratches across painted wood. Let listeners hear the pencil skip three times. Alma is trying to trace one of the height marks from memory. It was made with a carpenter's pencil held sideways. She can't get the width right.

INEZ: What did you write beside each line?

ALMA: Date. Initial. Shoes or socks. You let a child wear boots once and every measurement afterward turns into an argument.

INEZ: Who was tallest?

ALMA: My sister. Came round at forty-six and insisted. Took her hair down so nobody could say she cheated. People keep asking me about the children on that door. I want my sister's line back too.

INEZ: From the room above Vega Tyre, this is Mile 86. I'm Inez Flores. Alma Reyes has been trying to report a missing door. The form asks what the door would cost to replace. We've spent most of a Tuesday trying to answer.

**03:00–11:00 · The price of a door.** Record a visit to the builders' merchant. Counterman Wes quotes for a hollow door, then a solid one, then a jamb. He sets each sample on the counter so listeners hear the difference. Alma realizes none includes hinges. Wes remembers the family because her late husband always bought three hinges, although their little house only needed two. Alma corrects him. The third was for the screen. Close with Wes stamping an estimate and writing “pencil marks: by owner” in the labour column. Let the conversation move through the things he can price before Alma asks for the one he can't.

**11:00–16:00 · The Three-Hinge Waltz.** Five-minute original instrumental. Begin with three gently tapped notes from the actual hinges, pass the rhythm to brushed snare and upright piano, and let a fiddle take the tune. At the end Wes tries to play the three notes again. He gets them in a different order.

**16:00–25:00 · The kitchen drawer.** Alma, her grown son Nico and Inez empty one drawer in Nico's apartment. Stage this as a real-time sorting game. Each person picks something the other must identify by sound alone. A loose measuring spoon fools everyone. A bread-bag clip opens the memory of counting out sandwich portions at the motel. Spend time on how the motel worked: toast on an iron, drawer as a crib, freezer bags of ice passed from room to room. Nico has kept the room key. Alma is startled because she spent years feeling guilty about their unpaid key deposit. For the first time in the episode they laugh at the same thing.

NICO: I didn't steal it. The man said keep it until you get home.

ALMA: Well, where do you think you are now?

[He sets the key on his own kitchen table. Hold the silence long enough to hear the refrigerator switch off.]

**25:00–32:00 · The salvage auction.** Follow retired auction caller Fern through an empty sale barn. She reconstructs a sale from an old lot list, first in her working cadence, then in ordinary speech. Lot 41 was “mixed household timber.” Doors, by another name. She recalls separating a child's chair from the pile. Nobody bid. She bought it for a dollar and left it outside. Somebody collected it before dark. Inez asks whether that counts as returning it. Fern says she hasn't earned the right to pick the verb. She reads three more lots, slowly this time.

**32:00–38:00 · Rooms at Noon.** Six-minute sound portrait of Nico's kitchen and the motel laundry as each space goes from occupied to empty. A sparse guitar repeats the three hinge notes, one octave lower. End on the motel proprietor folding a fitted sheet and laughing when it comes out crooked. This is the episode's quiet driving stretch.

**38:00–47:00 · Lost property calls.** Three staged calls, about three minutes each, with a short host exchange. First, a woman describes a blue casserole dish, then withdraws the claim because the chip is on the wrong lip. Second, two brothers remember their father's toolbox as different colours. Under the red drawer liner it is green; both remember repainting it with him in different summers. Last, a caller wants to know how to return something privately. Inez offers the station's back step. The caller asks if the step is under cover when it rains. Leave that practical question hanging until the station break.

**47:00–55:00 · The back step.** Inez and Fern fit a small scrap-metal awning over the station step. Build the scene around their work: measuring twice, discovering the wall slopes, fetching a shorter screw, trying the awning with a watering can. Fern tells why she kept the auction ledger. Her father's name appears among the buyers. He bought the doors for firewood. Some had already been rescued before he arrived, and Fern has never found out by whom. They finish the awning. Inez records a second station announcement giving the step's hours. No names required.

**55:00–60:00 · The envelope.** Return to Alma's kitchen. Nobody has found her door. The anonymous caller has left something wrapped in a dish towel. It is the missing brass strike plate. Alma proves it by drawing a thumbnail over the groove left by a latch that never hung straight. She asks Inez to record the sound for her sister. End with a reprise of the waltz and this exchange over its last chord.

INEZ: Shall I put the estimate in the envelope too?

ALMA: Give it here. I need the back.

INEZ: What for?

ALMA: Directions to your step. There's a blue dish in my cupboard belongs to some poor woman with a very particular chip.

## Episode two — The Six-Ten Never Ran on Sundays

**00:00–04:00 · Cold open.** Sit with retired driver Del Mercer on a bench beside a road with no bus service. He has brought a thermos and two cups. He looks at his watch once and puts it away.

DEL: You picked Sunday.

INEZ: You said six-ten.

DEL: I said I'd be here at six-ten. People hear a time from a man with a bus licence, they put a vehicle underneath him.

INEZ: Why'd you come, then?

DEL: You were going to be standing out here with one cup.

[Pour both cups. A bird repeats a squeaky two-note call. Use it as the theme pickup.]

INEZ: This is Mile 86. Twelve years after the Mercy Cut washout, somebody is still replacing the paper timetable at the old bus stop. Today we brought the person who wrote it and the person who says it was always wrong.

**04:00–12:00 · Draw the route.** At a diner, Del draws the old route on the back of a paper placemat. Baker Lorna draws hers on another. Del starts at the depot. Lorna starts at her flat, crosses two unlit streets, waits outside the motel, rides to the depot, then walks another fifteen minutes to the ovens. He calls it a twenty-minute ride. She calls it the first hour of her working day. Compare each landmark. The strangest disagreement is a stop called the Orchard. Del remembers trees. Lorna remembers a girl called Peach whose dad waited with her in the rain.

**12:00–18:00 · Bench Seat.** Six-minute instrumental for muted bass, guitar harmonics and a softly brushed floor tom. Base the pulse on the pace of Lorna's walk. Open with her counting out the last twelve steps to the bakery. In the middle, let the bass drop away for one full verse as if somebody is waiting to cross. Bring the pulse back at exactly the same pace.

**18:00–27:00 · The Sunday ticket.** Inez visits ticket collector Edie, who has a shoebox organized by punched holes. Have her teach the host to read a ticket by touch. The oval punch marks a transfer. The star means a child rode free. One ticket is dated Sunday. Del insists there was no service. Edie admits she punched it herself when she was ten. For years she and her cousin played bus in an abandoned sedan. They issued each other tickets to real places and made up the people they might meet there. Reconstruct one whole imaginary run. The last passenger is a woman carrying a birthday cake perfectly level on both palms. Her stop is always just around the next corner because Edie never wants her to get off.

**27:00–34:00 · Six minutes late.** Lorna and Del walk the stretch from the old stop to the bakery in real time for the first six minutes. Intercut his view of the pavement with hers. He sees a straight line. She points out the gutter that filled to the kerb, the gate that swung across the footpath and the dark window she used as a mirror to tie her hair. Give the seventh minute to Del telling her he used to idle for six minutes at the depot after her connection left. He had called it recovery time. He had never asked who needed it.

**34:00–40:00 · Before the Ovens.** Six-minute field recording inside Lorna's bakery before opening. A dough hook, oven fan and metal peel supply the rhythm for a quiet organ piece. Halfway through she cuts the motors to listen for a trapped cricket, finds it beneath a sack and carries it outside. Leave the last half-minute in the open air.

**40:00–49:00 · The man who changes the paper.** The fresh timetable comes from Ellis at the weigh station. He does it because the shelter roof leaks and the plastic sleeve is just the right size to cover the hole behind it. That mundane explanation disappoints Inez until Ellis turns the paper over. On the back is a list of people with cars and the days they go into town. Record a ride with one of them, Manny, who takes a neighbour to collect new glasses. Manny asks for a fare of one fresh joke per trip. The neighbour has been saving one for a month. When she forgets the punch line he gives her a transfer for next week.

**49:00–56:00 · One seat each way.** Back at the diner, put Del and Lorna in charge of writing a one-day trial timetable with a single vehicle, two passenger seats and enough fuel for one return trip. Give them three requests: a baker starting early, a patient who can't arrive before nine, a daughter visiting after her father's lunch. Let them disagree in full. Lorna offers her ride to the patient. Del objects because the baker always gives up her seat. They finally ask the daughter, who says her father would actually prefer breakfast. One phone call changes the whole route. Leave a minute for Lorna to read the revised times back and catch Del forgetting the walk from the stop.

**56:00–60:00 · The return fare.** Del pins the trial sheet up with the times facing the bench. He writes the arrival times as large as the departures. Inez asks whether he intends to drive again. Give him room to take his coat off before he answers.

DEL: No. Cataract in the left eye. It makes every light look like a dandelion.

INEZ: Then where do you want me to put your name?

DEL: First passenger. Eye clinic. And put me down for coming home. Everybody likes writing the bit where a person gets somewhere.

[Paper slides into the sleeve. One cup unscrews, then the other. Bring back the walking tune for the final two minutes and end on the two-note bird.]

INEZ: Sunday's forecast is dry. There's room on the bench if it's wrong. From the tyre shop, good night.
