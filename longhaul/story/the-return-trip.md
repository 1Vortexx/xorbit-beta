# The Return Trip
Campaign treatment for Long Haul

When Mercy Cut washed out twelve years ago, June Vale drove the last relief truck east. The county played her radio call at the inquiry. “Mercy empty. Returning to depot.” Now June is dead. Her daughter Kit has inherited the garage, the pickup and a stack of bills. On the garage phone a woman has left a message: “My name is Alma Reyes. Your mother still has my front door. I'd like it back.”

## 1. The door
The first delivery is an actual front door, yellow, with children's heights pencilled up one jamb. It has spent twelve years as June's workbench. Kit must clear the tools, undo the brackets, load it in the pickup and secure it. The starter battery is weak. June has left a better one under the bench with a warranty dated yesterday. Let that be the first thing Kit learns about her mother after the funeral.

At a disused motel Alma picks out the door by the dent her son made learning to throw a horseshoe. Mercy's houses were demolished after the evacuation. There is nowhere to hang it. Alma wants it for the memorial supper on Saturday. Set it across two trestles. She lays plates on it and sets one fewer than there are chairs. “There's always somebody brings their own.”

## 2. The second load
A mechanic named Ellis has come to the supper on foot. Give him a lift to the old weigh station. He was seventeen at the time of the flood, too young for a commercial licence and old enough for everybody to ask him for favours. He remembers June coming back west after her famous last run. His proof is a carbon weigh ticket with a torn corner. At the station find the corresponding stub and weigh the empty pickup to bring the dead printer back to life. June's truck returned carrying 1,860 pounds. The official log says zero.

Kit thinks it was a rescue. Ellis says, “A truck full of people talks. I walked beside that thing for half a mile. All I heard was dishes.”

## 3. What the road costs
Deliver flour, lamp fuel and a box of stamped envelopes to families scattered along the old detour. Each delivery buys a small piece of the story, never a mandatory payment. A former tenant gives Kit a key. A former deputy lets her read his notebook but keeps it because the next page has his late wife's grocery list. At a roadblock the crew recognizes June's truck. Handle the toll, robbery or pursuit through ordinary gameplay. If supplies are lost, the post office has spare envelopes and the families can share the flour. The campaign remembers who went without.

June's unofficial load was people's belongings. The county paid to evacuate residents but declared everything left behind flood debris. June took doors, pots, photographs and the parts of houses people could carry. She also signed the clearance that let the demolition begin.

## 4. The missing eleven minutes
At the relay shack, tune two receivers and match their recordings by the sound of a wrench falling. One microphone caught June at the depot. The other caught the dispatcher at Mercy. Both recordings are authentic. The dispatcher's question was “Mercy truck, how's your bed?” June answered “Mercy empty. Returning to depot.” The county filed it as a population count. The dispatcher tried twice to correct the report. On the second try June heard him. She kept driving. She had promised one more family she'd be back before the bulldozer.

Alma's son was the man who brought that family out. He is alive, sells insurance, and would dearly love his mother to stop giving strangers his number. Let Kit call him. When asked if June saved them, he answers, “She brought back the good skillet. I saved us. Can we leave it there?”

## 5. Saturday service
A seasonal bus permit is up for renewal. The permit officer will ride the proposed route once, Saturday morning. Reaching the depot fastest proves very little. The player has to run a route people can actually use. Pick stops from the motel, camp and garage. Budget fuel, reserve a passenger seat, and post the departure times before leaving. At each stop a rider has an ordinary need: a shift, a prescription pickup, a visit to a sister who no longer drives. Waiting for a late rider may cost the connection. A breakdown opens a roadside repair or a lift to the next stop. The permit records the route actually completed.

At the terminus Kit decides what to do with June's clearance. File the full recording publicly, with the families' names removed. Give each family its own copy and leave publication to them. Or sign a correction in Kit's own name and keep June's voice private. Each choice changes who calls the garage afterward. None erases June's signature.

## Last image
Back at the garage the yellow door is standing upright. Alma has fitted a small brass bell to it. There is still no wall. The first passenger walks around the door, reaches back, and rings the bell anyway. Cut to the regular morning start-up routine. In free roam the stops Kit served have handwritten timetables. The first fare is a jar of peaches.

The radio episodes below were assembled before Kit inherited the garage. Either can play first, including before the campaign. The host knows the public record and the people, but has never heard the relay tapes. Campaign clues remain available through play.
